Exposé

Gödel’s mathematical results, namely the incompleteness theorems, posed a challenge to Turing. The question at issue is “whether there is a rigorous argument from these results to the claim that machines can never replace mathematicians or, more generally, that the human mind infinitely surpasses any finite machine” (Sieg 2013: 183). In the following essay the question concerned with the superiority of the human mind over machines will be looked at philosophically and in form of a historical and textual approach. Due to their relevance to the topic, the work of Lucas and Penrose will also be considered. Can man be replaced by computing machines or is man “a computing machine with a spirit”, as Gödel famously said? ___________________________________________________________________________

Introduction

When it comes to the question of computability and the mind, the work of Alan Turing and Kurt Gödel takes center stage in this discussion. The theory of computability goes back to the 1930’s. It was a result of the continuation of investigations in the subject of the foundations of mathematics, carried out by thinkers such as Gottlob Frege, Alfred North Whitehead, Bertrand Russel, David Hilbert, Paul Bernays, and John von Neumann. “The concept of an algorithm, or an effective or computable procedure, was central during these decades of foundational study, although for a long time no attempt was made to characterize the intuitive concept formally”. In the 1930’s a group of young mathematicians and logicians by the names of Emil Post (1897-1954), Kurt Gödel (1906-1978), Alonzo Church (1903-1995), and Alan Turing (1912-1954) changed that and established the theoretical basis for computability, which reshaped and revolutionized the mathematical world and gave birth to the Information Age. In the following paper, the contemporary work of John Lucas (born 1929) and Roger Penrose (born 1931) is included because of its relevance for the question of computability and the mind. These two figures are best known in recent times for arguing that Gödels incompleteness theorems imply that human minds are not computing machines. The main research questions of the paper are following:

How do the works of Emil Post, Alan Turing and Kurt Gödel compare concerning the question whether the human mind is a machine or not?

What are the differences between Turing and Gödel when it comes to the question of the computability of the human brain?

What role have the concepts of creativity, learning and intuition to play  concerning the question of minds and machines?

What is said in works of John Lucas and Roger Penrose in the contemporary debate about minds and machines and how do these statements compare to the works of Turing and Gödel?



These questions will be looked at in a historical and textual approach.

Emil Post and his Anticipation of Turing’s and Gödel’s Work

Although today Gödel is famous for the incompleteness theorem, similar work has been done as early as the 1920’s by Post, who isn’t known to a wider audience. To start off, we have a look at this fascinating chapter in the history of science.

Post was an immigrant from Poland and a graduate student in mathematical logic at Columbia from 1917 to 1920. He lost his left arm when quite young and suffered attacks of manic-depressive illness quite frequently during his life. “To avert the manic episodes, Post would give himself two problems to work on, switching off the one that was going well when he found himself becoming too excited. This did not always work, however, and Post often received the electroshock treatment that was thought effective in those days”. He died from a heart attack at the early age of 57 shortly after such a treatment. In 1920-1921 Post held a post-doctoral fellowship at Princeton, during which he tried to analyze the whole Principia Mathematica “with a view to proving its completeness and consistency as he had done for propositional calculus. […] He showed that all theorems of Principia (and probably of any conceivable symbolic logic) could be derived by simple systems of rules he called normal systems”. This looked like a great step forward at first, but as he struggled to analyze even the simplest normal systems he realized that the situation was the opposite of what he had first thought: he had merely distilled the complexity of the Principia into a smaller system.

Post claimed, that sometime in 1921 he caught a glimpse of the true situation:

Normal systems can simulate any symbolic logic, indeed any mechanical system for deriving theorems.

This means, however, that all such systems can be mechanically listed, and the diagonal argument then shows that the general problem of deciding whether a given theorem is produced by a given system is unsolvable.

It follows, in turn, that no consistent mechanical system can produce all theorems.

These findings include (in different form) the discoveries of Turing on the nature of computability and unsolvability, and Gödel’s theorem on the incompleteness of formal systems for mathematics.

Gödel published his incompleteness theorem in 1931 and Church stated the Church’s thesis in 1935. This thesis proposes a definition of computability and implies the existence of unsolvable problems. Both propositions will be looked at in more detail later in the paper.

“Church’s definition of computability was not immediately convincing (at least not to Gödel), and some equivalent definitions were proposed soon after. The one that convinced Gödel was Turing’s [On computable numbers, with an application to the Entscheidungsproblem], now known as the Turing machine. Post’s normal systems, another equivalent of the computability concept, were still unpublished [by 1935]”. After the publication of Post’s findings in 1936 he met Gödel in 1938 and tried to tell him his story and that he had already found the consequences of Turing’s definition of computation. The next day after the meeting, Post wrote a postcard to Gödel that reads as follows:

“I am afraid that I took advantage of you on this, I hope but our first meeting. But for fifteen years I had carried around the thought of astounding the mathematical world with my unorthodox ideas, and meeting the man chiefly responsible for the vanishing of that dream rather carried me away.

Since you seemed interested in my way of arriving at these new developments perhaps Church can show you a longer letter I wrote to him about them. As for any claims I might make perhaps the best I can say is that I would have proved Gödel’s theorem in 1921 – had I been Gödel”.

Post wrote a couple more letters to Gödel after that and Gödel replied, courteously assuring Post that he had not regarded Post’s claims as egotistical, and that he had found Post’s approach interesting – however, Gödel did not take the matter any further. “In 1941, Post made another attempt to tell his story, in a long and rambling paper ‘Absolutely unsolvable problems and relatively undecidable propositions – an account of an anticipation’ submitted to the American Journal of Mathematics. The stream-of-consciousness style of parts of the paper and lack of formal detail made it unpublishable in such a journal”. In this paper Post reached the conclusion that “mathematical thinking is, and must be, essentially creative”. This fact, he says, “makes of the mathematician much more than a kind of clever being who can do quickly what a machine could do ultimately. We see that a machine would never give a complete logic; for once the machine is made, we could prove a theorem it does not prove”. As a consequence of his own work, Post became increasingly interested in the “psychological analysis of the mental process involved in combinatory mathematical processes”.

Despite the setbacks Post continued his research and his most influential work was yet to come – in 1943 he was able to introduce his groundbreaking theory of recursively enumerable sets to the American Mathematical Society. Another short paper titled the “Post correspondence problem” followed in 1945, an unsolvable problem with many applications to the theory of computation. In 1947 Post showed the unsolvability of an even more fundamental problem about semigroups – called the “Word Problem”.

“Thus Post should be celebrated, not only for his fundamental work in logic, but also for constructing a bridge between logic and classical mathematics. Few people today cross that bridge, but perhaps if Post’s work were better known, more would be encouraged to make the journey”. With this in mind, we now move on to Turing and Gödel. If one wants to get more information about Post and his anticipation of Gödel and Turing, for example the in-detail differences between Post and Gödel, Stillwell’s paper that is cited here marks a good starting point.



The Question of Consciousness and Thinking Machines

The largest problem and center question when it comes to minds and machines is whether the human brain is a computer or not. Consciousness is somehow related to this question because the human brain either generates or receives it. But consciousness is still a big mystery in science and leads to a lot of confusion in scientific and philosophical debates. Can one say that a computer that passes the Turing test is conscious? Quite possibly not. “According to Turing, thinking is one thing, consciousness another”. Turing himself wondered whether consciousness can be programmed into existence – but he also said that there are mysteries about it. “Jack Good once asked Turing (in about 1950) whether there are any circumstances in which he would say that a computer is conscious. Turing replied, ‘I would say it was conscious if otherwise I would be punished!’ Turing’s view seems to have been that although the questions ‘Can computers think?’ and ‘Can computers pass the Turing test?’ are close enough, the questions ‘Can computers pass the test and ‘Can computers be conscious?’ are very different from one another.” For this reason, we leave consciousness out of the tricky question whether the human mind is a machine or not and concentrate on the question of thinking machines in this paper.



Gödel on Turing’s “Philosophical Error”

To start off: “Gödel himself is widely believed to have held that minds are more powerful than machines, whereas Turing is usually said to have taken the opposite position. In fact, neither of these characterizations is much more than a caricature. The actual picture is subtle and complex”.

In the introductory section of his doctoral thesis of 1929 Gödel already mentions the possibility of the incompleteness of axiomatic formal systems of mathematics: “[…] we cannot at all exclude out of hand…a proof of the unsolvability of a problem if we observe that what is at issue here is only unsolvability by certain precisely stated formal means of inference”. After the announcement of the incompleteness theorems in 1931, other remarks of Gödel show, that he “was inclined from an early date to separate the question of mechanical decidability from decidability by human reason”. He states, for example, that:

“The generalized undecidability results do not establish any bounds for the powers of human reason, but rather for the potentialities of pure formalisms in mathematics… Turing’s analysis of mechanically computable functions is independent of the question whether there exist finite non-mechanical procedures… such as involve the use of abstract terms on the basis of their meaning”.

The procedures, that Gödel speaks of here, are finite – they are evidently not mechanical, because they involve the use of “abstract terms on the basis of their meaning”. Sometime in the 1930’s Gödel says that it is not possible to mechanize mathematical reasoning. “It is not possible to replace the human mathematician by a machine, even if we confine ourselves to number-theoretic problems”. In the 1951 Gibbs Lecture, the central implication of the incompleteness theorems is formulated as a disjunction:

“Either mathematics is incomplete in this sense, that its evident axioms can never be comprised in a finite rule, that is to say, the human mind (even within the realm of pure mathematics) infinitely surpasses the powers of any finite machine, or else there exist absolutely unsolvable Diophantine problems”.

In some notes in the Nachlass Gödel says that he wants to show that the human mind does in fact infinitely surpass the powers of any finite machine. For example, Gödel states “that in the systematic establishment of axioms of mathematics, new axioms again and again become evident, even though they do not follow by formal logic from those previously established. The incompleteness theorems do not, however, exclude the possibility that every clearly posed yes or no question is solvable through such a development of new axioms, for what a machine cannot imitate is the fact that new axioms become evident to us on the basis of the (abstract) meaning of the primitive concepts”. This view of mathematical knowledge differs in some respect from the view that results from Turing’s analysis – but this point will be made clear later in the paper.

When asked about the philosophical implications of his incompleteness theorems Gödel wrote the following response in a draft letter in 1962 – a nice summation of his view:

“My theorems only show that the mechanization of mathematics, i.e. the elimination of the mind and of abstract entities, is impossible, if one wants to have a satisfactory foundation and system of mathematics.

I have not proved that there are mathematical questions undecidable for the human mind, but only that there is no machine (or blind formalism) that can decide all number theoretic questions (even of a certain very special kind).

Likewise it does not follow from my theorems that there are no convincing consistency proofs for the usual mathematical formalisms. What is practically certain is that there are, for the classical formalisms, no conclusive combinatorial consistency proofs (such as Hilbert expected to give), i.e., no consistency proofs that use only concepts referring to finite combinations of symbols and not referring to any infinite totality of such combinations.

…It is not the structure itself of the deductive systems which is being threatened with a breakdown, but only a certain interpretation of it, namely its interpretation as a blind formalism.

… our knowledge of the abstract mathematical entities themselves (as opposed to the formalisms corresponding to them) is in a deplorable state. This is not surprising in view of the fact that the prevailing bias even denies their existence”.

Human reason (or the “monad”, as Gödel calls it sometimes) may be able to use abstract terms on the basis of their meaning, as Gödel suggests, “whereas this is not a possibility in the case of a purely formalistic or mechanistic view of mathematics”. In 1972 in a late passage, Gödel adds that human reason is capable of constantly developing its understanding of abstract terms. He called it “A philosophical error in Turing’s work”:

“Turing in his 1937…gives an argument which is supposed to show that mental procedures cannot go beyond mechanical procedures. However, this argument is inconclusive. What Turing disregards completely is the fact that mind, in its use, is not static, but constantly developing, i.e., that we understand abstract terms more and more precisely as we go on using them, and that more and more abstract terms enter the sphere of our understanding”.

In 1974 Gödel gave a different version of the same note to Hao Wang, a mathematician, logician and philosopher who later became a commentator on Gödel, in which Gödel explicitly says that Turing’s argument involves “the supposition that finite mind is capable of only a finite number of distinguishable states”.

Copeland and Shagrir argue, that Turing can be readily defended against Gödel’s charge of philosophical error. Gödel’s criticism that “Turing disregards completely… the fact that mind in its use is not static, but constantly developing”, seems entirely misdirected if one takes a closer look at Turing’s work. Gödel’s claim that Turing put forward an argument supposed to show that mental procedures cannot go beyond mechanical procedures, goes back to Turing’s famous paper “On Computable Numbers”. But there is no such argument to be found in this paper. “Turing, on the page discussed by Gödel, was not talking about the general scope of mental procedures; he was addressing a different question, namely, ‘What are the possible processes which can be carried out in computing a number?’ Furthermore, there is a passage in ‘On Computable Numbers’ that seemingly runs counter to the view attributed to Turing by Gödel. Having defined a certain infinite binary sequence δ, which he shows to be uncomputable, Turing says: ‘It is (so far as we know present) possible that any assigned number of figures of δ can be calculated, but not by a uniform process. When sufficiently many figures of δ have been calculated, an essentially new method is necessary in order to obtain more figures (1936, 79)”. This passage is interesting, because Turing suggests the possibility that the human mathematician can calculate any desired number of digits of an uncomputable sequence – by virtue of creating new methods when necessary.

To follow this up, Turing did not say that “a finite mind is capable of not only a finite number of distinguishable states”. He said: “We will… suppose that the number of states of mind which need to be taken into account [for the purpose of analyzing computability] is finite”. Turing acknowledges that beyond these states there may be “more complicated states of mind”, “but points out that, again for the purpose of analyzing computability, reference to these complicated states ‘can be avoided by writing more symbols on the tape’ (Turing 1936, 76)”. We have to point out, that Turing is in effect distinguishing between elementary states of mind and complex states of mind. He notes that symbols on the tape can serve as surrogate for complex states of mind. “Turing nowhere suggests that the ‘more complicated states of mind’ are finite in number (and nor does he suggest that they fail to be distinguishable unless finite in number)”.



Mathematical Intuition and Turing

In the last chapter we showed that Turing’s text “On Computable Numbers” does not support Gödel’s interpretation. In this chapter we have a look at Turing’s publication “Systems of Logic Based on Ordinals”, which was published in the year 1939 and which furthers Gödel’s apparent misinterpretation of Turing. In this publication Turing states:

“In pre-Gödel times it was thought by some that… all the intuitive judgments of mathematics could be replaced by a finite number of… [formal] rules. … In consequence of the impossibility of finding a formal logic which wholly eliminates the necessity of using intuition, we naturally turn to “non-constructive” systems of logic with which not all the steps in a proof are mechanical, some being intuitive”.

This paragraph shows that in Turing’s view, the “faculty of intuition” brings it about that mathematical “judgements” exceed what can be expressed by means of a single formal system. He said that “the activity of the intuition consists in making spontaneous judgements which are not the result of conscious trains of reasoning”. Copeland and Shagrir state that “Turing’s cheerful use of mentalistic vocabulary in this connection makes it very unlikely that Gödel was correct in finding an argument in the 1936 paper supposedly showing that mental procedures cannot go beyond mechanical procedures”.

Some remarks about Turing’s thinking about intuition can be found in letters which he wrote during the early part of the war to Max Newman – for example:

“I think you take a much more radically Hilbertian attitude about mathematics than I do. You say ‘If all this whole formal outfit is not about finding proofs which can be checked on a machine it’s difficult to know what it is about.’ When you say ‘on a machine’ do you have in mind that there is (or should be or could be, but has not been actually described anywhere) some fixed machine on which proofs are to be checked, and that the formal outfit is, as it were about this machine. If you take this attitude (and it is this one that seems to me so extreme Hilbertian [sic]) there is little more to be said: we simply have to get used to the technique of this machine and resign ourselves to the fact that there are some problems to which we can never get the answer. On these lines my ordinal logics would make no sense. However I don’t think you really hold quite this attitude because you admit that in the case of the Gödel example one can decide that the formula is true i.e. you admit that there is a fairly definite idea of a true formula which is quite different from the idea of a provable one. Throughout my paper on ordinal logics I have been assuming this too. … If you think of various machines I don’t see your difficulty. One imagines different machines allowing different sets of proofs, and by choosing a suitable machine one can approximate “truth” by “provability” better than with a less suitable machine, and can in a sense approximate it as well as you please. The choice of a … machine involves intuition …”

In this letter Turing describes what is called the Multi-Machine picture of mathematics (term by Copeland and Shagrir). The role of intuition is described very precisely in this picture. “Intuition is responsible for the selection of the appropriate theorem-proving machine (the appropriate Turing machine), and the rest is mechanical. The intuition involved in selecting the appropriate theorem-proving machine is, Turing said, ‘interchangeable’ with the intuition involved in finding a proof of the theorem”.

At this stage we can go back to Post and remember his statement from the second chapter: “The logical process is essentially creative. This conclusion ... makes of the mathematician much more than a kind of clever being who can do quickly what a machine could do ultimately. We see that a machine would never give a complete logic; for once the machine is made we could prove a theorem it does not prove”. Both, Turing and Post, suggest a creativity and intuition that go beyond the workings of a single theorem-proving machine. While Post went on with an interest in the “psychological analysis of the mental process involved in combinatory mathematical processes”, Turing kept his research on these questions more formal (for example his late work on the topic of artificial life).



The Multi-Machine Picture of Mind and Mathematics



The figure above shows a diagrammatic representation of the Multi-Machine theory of mind as created by Copeland and Shagrir. The y-axis shows enumerated Turing machines, “beginning with the first (relative to some unspecified underlying ordering of the Turing machines)”. The x-axis shows successive stages of development of learning mind M. “M successively mutates (in discrete jumps) from one theorem-proving Turing machine into another. Idealizing away death and other contingent resource-constraints, we can imagine the trajectory continuing indefinitely to the right”.

We must ask the question, what exactly Turing means by the idea of learning. “A human mathematician working according to the rules of a fixed logical system is in effect a proof-producing machine; and when intuition supplies the mathematician with some new means of proof, he or she becomes a different proof-producing machine, capable of a larger set of proofs”. How is this transformation of the mathematician achieved? In the writings of the post-war Turing, the term “intuition” drops from view. Instead the post-war Turing in writing about the mind uses the idea of learning – “in the sense of devising or discovering new methods of proof”.

Here are some examples, where Turing emphasizes the topic of learning:

“[I]ntellectual activity consists mainly of various kinds of search”.

“[T]his danger of the mathematician making mistakes is an unavoidable corollary of his power of sometimes hitting upon an entirely new method”.

“[O]ne feature that … should be incorporated … is a ‘random element’. … This would result in the behavior of the machine not being by any means completely determined by the experiences to which it was subjected”.

“What we want is a machine that can learn from experience. The possibility of letting the machine alter its own instructions provides the mechanisms for this. … One can imagine that after the machine had been operating for some time, the instructions would have altered out of all recognition”.

The last point can be called instruction-modification and is central for the Multi-Machine theory of mind, the transformation of one Turing machine into another. “Modifying the table of instructions in effect transforms the learning machine into a different Turing machine. So a machine with the ability to learn is able to traverse the space of proof-finding Turing machines. The learning machine successively mutates from one proof-finding machine into another, becoming capable of wider sets of proofs as it searches for and acquires new, more powerful methods of proof”.

When we go back to the diagram, the question becomes whether the function from mind-stages to Turing machines is computable, or rather, how could this function fail to be computable. Gödel never commented specifically on the Multi-Machine theory of the human mind, but he commented on a concept he called a “race” of theorem-proofing machines, which is analogous to the mind stages of the Multi-Machine theory:

“Such a state of affairs would show that there is something nonmechanical in the sense that the overall plan for the historical development of machines is not mechanical. If the general plan is mechanical, then the whole race can be summarized in one machine”.

Gödel did not specify what he meant by the notion of an “overall plan” and this doesn’t help very much with the specific question of where the uncomputability might come from. Copeland and Shagrir state, that one can only guess how Turing would have answered if questioned on this point. We know that Turing gave emphasis on the inclusion of a random element in the learning process. So he might have stated that the source of the uncomputability is randomness. “The core idea is that the partially random learning machine emulates the “activity of the intuition” in its walk through the space of proof-finding Turing machines. Heuristic and other forms of search, together with learning algorithms and instruction modification, coupled with the activity from time to time of a random element, produce the evolving sequence of proof-finding Turing machines described by the Multi-Machine theory”. Turing spoke in several occasions about the inclusion of a random element in a learning machine. He emphasized, for example, that the same process of search involving a random element “is used in the analogous process of evolution”.





Closing Thoughts on Gödel and Turing

In conclusion we can say that Gödel’s critical note on Turing, namely “Turing’s Philosophical Error”, is of interest not because Turing committed such an error about the mind machine issue, “but because of the light that the critical note helps to shed on the similarities, and the differences, in the views of these two great founders of the study of computability”. On one hand, Gödel seemed to have misinterpreted some of Turing’s statements about the analysis of computability as claims about the mind in general. On the other hand, “Gödel praised Turing’s analysis of computability but objected to the thought that Turing’s restrictive conditions are grounded in facts about human cognition”.

To state it precisely: Turing agreed with Gödel that the mind is more powerful than any given Turing machine. He didn’t think, however, that the mind is something other than machinery. This is where Turing differs from Gödel.



Penrose on Uncomputability and the Brain

Although the Penrose’s thesis that will be discussed in this chapter contains some remarks on human consciousness, we still include his contemporary thinking in the matter of minds and machines in this chapter. The Penrose’s thesis is the claim that the action of the brain is hypercomputational. Penrose suggests in various of his monographs and papers that the brain’s uncomputability is key to explaining the phenomenon of consciousness. “According to Penrose, the brain’s hypercomputational action, and the role this plays in generating conscious experience, will not be fully understood until the advent of what he calls the New Theory in physics: He says that ‘hypercomputational actions’ in the brain are the ‘non-computable effects of [the] New Theory’. This ‘presumed New Theory’, he says, goes ‘beyond current quantum mechanics’: It is ‘presently unknown in detail’ and involves ‘hiertho undiscovered’ laws’.

The argument for this thesis is based on Gödel’s incompleteness theorems. Penrose states for example: "[H]uman intuition and insight ... cannot be reduced to any set of computational rules. ... Gödel's theorem indeed shows this, and provides the foundation of my argument that there must be more to human thinking than can ever be achieved by a computer." He also states that he regards Gödel’s incompleteness theorems “as providing a strong case for human understanding being something essentially non-computable”.

The same line of argument goes back to the philosopher John Lucas (1961) and is often called the “Gödel Argument” – although it was anticipated as early as 1921 by Post as we have seen in the second chapter. Penrose calls it the “Gödel-Turing-Argument” and Turing himself called it the “Mathematical Objection”, of which there is the following summary:

“Recently the theorem of Gödel and related results ... have shown that if one tries to use machines for such purposes as determining the truth or falsity of mathematical theorems and one is not willing to tolerate an occasional wrong result, then any given machine will in some cases be unable to give an answer at all. On the other hand the human intelligence seems to be able to find methods of ever-increasing power for dealing with such problems[,] 'transcending' the methods available to machines.”

Turing also states the same argument in somewhat more detail in his 1950 article ‘Computing Machinery and Intelligence”: “The questions that we know the machines must fail on are of this type, 'Consider the machine specified as follows . . . Will this machine ever answer "Yes" to any question?' The dots are to be replaced by a description of some machine in a standard form. When the machine described bears a certain comparatively simple relation to the machine which is under interrogation, it can be shown that the answer is either wrong or not forthcoming. This is the mathematical result: it is argued that it proves a disability of machines to which the human intellect is not subject”.

As we have seen in the previous chapters, Turing by no means endorsed the “Gödel-Turing Argument” – one of his objections was discussed in chapter 6, “The Multi-Machine Theory” as described by Copeland and Shagrir. Gödel’s view, as we have also seen, is formulated by him as a disjunction (footer No. 17). At the beginning of the 1970’s, Gödel recast this disjunction into an implication: “If my result [incompleteness] is taken together with the rationalistic attitude which Hilbert had and which was not refuted by my results, then [we can infer] the sharp result that mind is not mechanical. This is so, because, if the mind were a machine, there would, contrary to this rationalistic attitude, exist number-theoretic questions undecidable for the human mind”.

Hilbert’s “rationalistic attitude” means that “in mathematics there is no ignorabimus” – there is no mathematical question that in principle the mind is incapable of settling. The question becomes whether this “rationalistic attitude” could ever be established to be correct – “i.e., whether it could ever be established that there are no absolutely undecidable problems. It is, to be sure, difficult to see how this could ever be done”. Copeland, Shagrir and Sprevak execute a reduction ad absurdum concerning the Penrose’s argument, which successfully establishes that human mathematicians do not use a knowably sound Turing-machine algorithm in order to ascertain mathematical truth. “[H]is argument shows with equal success that human mathematicians, in ascertaining mathematical truth, do not use any knowably sound procedure that is capable of being executed by an oracle machine”.  Turing’s oracle machines (o-machines) are universal Turing machines equipped with at least one additional basic operation that no Turing machine proper can simulate. “As Turing explained, oracle machines form a hierarchy that extends ever upwards. Let the first-order o-machines be those whose oracle procedures values of the Turing-machine halting function H(x,y). The second-order o-machines are those with an oracle that can say whether or not any given first order o-machine eventually halts if set in motion with such-and-such a number inscribed on its tape, and so on for the third order and in general α-order o-machines. Penrose’s argument was originally marketed as showing that human understanding does not consist in any process that a Turing machine can execute…, but his argument is so powerful that it equally supports the conclusion that human understanding does not consist in any process that the richly hypercomputational oracle machines can execute. … Penrose noted this difficulty in his 1994 book Shadows of the Mind (p. 380)”: “The final conclusion of all this is rather alarming. For it suggests that we must seek a non-computable physical theory that reaches beyond every [recursive] level of oracle machines (and perhaps beyond). No doubt there are readers who believe that the last vestige of credibility of my argument has disappeared at this stage! I certainly should not blame any reader for feeling this way”.

His suggestion for a way out:

“[I]t need not be the case that human mathematical understanding is in principle as powerful as any oracle machine at all. … Thus, we need not necessarily conclude that the physical laws that we seek reach, in principle, beyond every computable level of oracle machine (or even reach the first order). We need only seek something that is not equivalent to any specific oracle machine”.

The reduction ad absurdum can be showed in the following way: “It is customary in recursion theory to say that problems of equal ‘hardness’ are of the same degree: Problems that are solvable by Turing machines are said to be of degree 0. Penrose seems to be suggesting that physical laws occupy a position in between degree 0 and degree 1 (the degree of problems that are solvable by a first-order oracle machine but not by a Turing machine)”. It is actually known that there are degrees between 0 and 1 – so the suggestion Penrose makes is coherent. However there is a problem: “Let i (for “intermediate”) be a degree between 0 and 1 and let I be the class of o-machines that are able to solve problems of degree I (and no harder problems). Do mathematicians use, in ascertaining mathematical truth, a knowably sound procedure able to be executed by a machine in I? Not if Penrose’s argument is sound, since it applies equally to o-machines in I. To borrow a phrase of Penrose’s (from his [2013, p. xxxiv]), the Gödel Argument involves a ‘never-ending capability of being able to ‘stand back’ and contemplate whatever structure had been considered previously’: Whatever structure – whatever physical system – is contemplated, the argument deems it not to be the mind”. In other words: The Gödel argument can simply be reapplied to show that human mathematicians aren’t a oracle machine either. It is impossible for Penrose to draw the circle and say that ‘There, that’s the mind!’, because the Gödel argument can be applied again to show that it’s not the mind.

The project of Penrose was to find the missing science of consciousness but in the end he concludes that: “[T]he Gödel diagonalization procedure can be applied to systems much more general than merely computational ones. Thus, my arguments would equally imply that our missing theory must be not just non-computational, but also beyond ... Turing's notion of oracle computation. ... It seems that the quality of 'understanding'—which is what this discussion is effectively all about—is something very mysterious.” Lucas concludes from the Gödel Argument that “no scientific enquiry can ever exhaust the … human mind”. And Gödel himself thought that the brain must be “a computing machine connected with a spirit”.

Copeland, Shagrir and Sprevak conclude that “unlike Gödel and Lucas, Penrose seems to think that there must be a fully physical account of consciousness, but he has failed to make it clear what physical conception of consciousness can possibly remain for one who endorses the Gödel Argument”. Only recently there were some notes from Gödel uncovered in the Nachlass that show what Gödel called his philosophical viewpoint:

“My philosophical viewpoint

1. The world is rational.

2. Human reason can, in principle, be developed more highly (through certain techniques).

3. There are systematic methods for the solutions of all problems (also art, etc).

4. There are other worlds and rational beings, who are of a different and higher kind.

5. The world in which we live is not the only one in which we shall live or have lived.

6. Incomparably more is knowable a priori than is currently known

7. The development of human thought since the Renaissance is thoroughly one--‐sided.

8. Reason in mankind will be developed on every side.

9. The formally correct is part of a science of reality.

10. Materialism is false.

11. The higher beings are connected to the others by analogy, not by composition.

12. Concepts have an objective existence (likewise mathematical theorems).

13. There is a scientific (exact) philosophy and theology (this is also more highly fruitful for science), which deals with the concepts of the highest abstractness.”

These viewpoints read almost in a mystical manner and the points about other worlds and higher beings can be compared to the works of mystics such as Rudolf Steiner for example. One has to point out that Gödel still believed in a rational world and that he held the position that there are systematic methods for the solutions of all problems.

To conclude with Penrose’s view on the brain in relationship to Turing, Penrose states: “It seems likely that he [Turing] viewed physical action in general – which would include the action of a human brain – to be always reducible to some kind of Turing-machine action”. Penrose named this claim the Turing thesis. Yet, as Copeland et al. show, Turing never endorsed this thesis and was aware that it might be false. “Turing was in fact a important forerunner of the modern debate concerning the possibility of uncomputability in physics and uncomputability in the action of the human brain. … In a 1951 lecture on BBC radio Turing suggested that it may not be possible for a computer to simulate the human brain because of the brain’s quantum-mechanical nature”. Thus we can state that the view that Turing claimed that the action of the brain must be computable is wrong.



Conclusion



1.  We have shown that Emil Post anticipated the works of Alan Turing and Kurt Gödel as early as the year 1921. His conclusion was that a machine can never give a complete logic and therefore man can never be replaced by a machine. His findings led Post to an interest in the “psychological analysis of the mental process involved in combinatory mathematical processes”.

2. We have discussed what Gödel called “Turing’s Philosophical Error” and have shown that this interpretation of Turing’s paper On Computable Numbers was a misinterpretation on the side of Gödel. We have seen that there are more similarities than differences between Gödel and Turing in these questions, contrary to popular belief.

3. We have seen that the concepts of intuition and learning play an important part in Turing’s thinking about minds and machines.

4. We have discussed the Multi-Machine picture of mind and mathematics and have shown that randomness seems to be the force that leads to problems in computability and the mind.

5. We have had a look at Penrose’s hypercomputational model of the human brain and have shown the problems that arise with this view. Namely, they lead right back to the work of Gödel and Turing.



To conclude: We have seen that there are still many open questions that need further exploration concerning the topic of minds and machines. The question, whether the human mind is computable and whether there can be thinking machines that can replace human mathematicians leads to further questions about the topics of randomness, creativity, intuition, learning and in the case of Gödel even about the spirit. Another open field is quantum mechanics and its connection to human consciousness. If logicians, mathematicians, physicists and philosophers of mind find a way to cooperate in these matters, many interesting discoveries await us.
